# Federal and Public-Service Plain Language

Use this profile for U.S. federal notices, instructions, benefit or service information, form text, compliance guidance, and other public-facing agency communication.

Do not apply it automatically to statutes, regulations, contracts, adjudicative decisions, or language whose legal form is itself controlling.

## Authorities

- [Plain Writing Act of 2010](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/COMPS-13907), which defines plain writing around clarity, concision, organization, subject matter, and intended audience.
- [Administrative Conference recommendation on plain-language drafting](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2017-12-29/pdf/2017-28124.pdf), including audience-centered organization, active voice, headings, lists, and familiar words.

A statute, regulation, required notice, approved form, agency-specific guide, or supplied legal review outranks this general profile.

## Reader and Task

Identify what the reader needs to know or do. When structure is in scope, present information in the order needed to act:

1. Who the information applies to.
2. What benefit, service, requirement, decision, or change is involved.
3. What the reader must or may do.
4. The deadline, effective date, required documents, and consequences.
5. Where to obtain help, submit material, appeal, or learn more when the source provides that information.

Do not create missing eligibility rules, deadlines, contact details, rights, remedies, or procedural steps.

## Plain-Language Style

- Address the reader directly when appropriate and identify the agency or decision-maker clearly.
- Put the main action or decision before background that the reader does not need first.
- Use familiar words while preserving statutory terms, program names, defined terms, and necessary technical language.
- Define an unfamiliar term or acronym on first use when the source supplies enough information to do so.
- Prefer active voice when it clarifies responsibility. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or legally inappropriate to infer.
- Use descriptive headings, lists, tables, and question-and-answer structure when they make conditions or steps easier to find.
- Keep related conditions, exceptions, deadlines, and consequences together.
- State dates, amounts, addresses, and actions consistently and unambiguously.
- Remove bureaucratic filler, duplicative background, unnecessary nominalizations, and internal process detail that the public does not need.

## Legal and Policy Fidelity

- Preserve eligibility conditions, exceptions, qualifications, deadlines, rights, obligations, discretion, and standards of proof.
- Preserve distinctions among `may`, `can`, `should`, `shall`, and `must`.
- Do not promise an outcome, benefit, timetable, appeal right, or agency action that the source does not promise.
- Do not broaden or narrow who is covered.
- Do not convert guidance into a requirement or a requirement into guidance.
- Do not remove a required disclosure merely because it is repetitive or formal.

When plainness and legal effect appear to conflict, preserve legal effect, complete safe edits, and report the unresolved passage in one sentence.
